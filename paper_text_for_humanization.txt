ABSTRACT

Fact-checkers need concise evidence sets, but a selector cannot recover evidence that retrieval misses and cannot make reliable set decisions from miscalibrated scores. We separate these failure sources in a retrieval-conditioned study on MOCHEG. Every method receives the same packet of 40 text and 20 image candidates. Six probability maps and four selection policies are evaluated on 1,490 locked validation claims across five deterministic training-role partitions. Retrieval is the dominant boundary: among 10,970 claims with released complete-gold evidence, the packet oracle reaches 0.3560 evidence F1, complete gold is present for 4.39%, and 3,086 claims contain no reachable gold item. Prior-aware calibration reduces mean Brier score from 0.0847 to 0.0330. Expected-F1 then returns 3.50 items per claim and reaches 0.1759 evidence F1, improving on fixed top-5 by 0.0085 with a claim-aligned 95% interval of [0.0030, 0.0140]. A calibrated threshold remains stronger at 0.1807. A post hoc reachability analysis localizes the expected-F1 benefit: it reaches 0.5240 F1 when complete gold is available in the packet, but offers no gain over fixed top-5 when gold is only partially reachable. Size and redundancy penalties select zero throughout, probability perturbations reverse the adaptive gain, and a shared verifier does not establish the declared non-inferiority margin. Calibrated expected-F1 therefore provides a precision-oriented operating point. It does not establish end-to-end superiority; calibrated thresholding performs better overall, and retrieval reachability remains the main limitation.

KEYWORDS

multimodal fact-checking, evidence retrieval, evidence selection, probability calibration, expected F1, reproducibility

1. INTRODUCTION

An evidence-based verdict is auditable only when a reader can inspect the material behind it. In the multimodal setting, that material may include sentences, documents, and images. Modern systems can retrieve a large candidate pool, but a fact-checker still needs a short set that is relevant enough to inspect and complete enough to support the decision. MOCHEG makes this problem explicit by pairing image-text claims with text evidence, image evidence, and three-way verdicts [CITE: yao2023mocheg].

Most pipelines choose the final evidence with a fixed top-k rule. This is easy to reproduce, but it assumes every claim needs the same number of items. Adaptive selectors instead use membership scores to choose a claim-specific set. A threshold returns every candidate whose score exceeds a cutoff. A count model predicts a set size. Expected-F1 decoding searches candidate prefixes for the size with the highest expected set F1. These rules share an important dependency: their scores must behave like probabilities. Ranking quality alone is insufficient. A monotone score transformation leaves the ranking unchanged while changing the threshold and expected-F1 decisions.

That dependency is especially risky under class imbalance. Evidence membership is rare within a retrieved packet, and balanced logistic training deliberately changes the effective class prior. Temperature scaling can change confidence but cannot correct a prior-induced intercept error. If this mismatch is overlooked, an expected-utility decoder may select too many candidates even when average precision improves.

Retrieval creates a second dependency. A selector cannot recover an item absent from its packet. Reporting selector F1 without the packet oracle can make a decoding error and a retrieval miss look identical. We therefore evaluate selection inside one fixed pipeline, shown in Fig. [REF: fig:architecture]. Every method receives the same 40 text and 20 image candidates, and all evidence-set scores use the released complete-gold mapping.

Figure caption: Study design and information boundaries. Retrieval fixes one packet per claim before gold labels are joined. Scoring, calibration, and policy selection use disjoint training roles. Every evidence policy is then evaluated on the same locked validation claims, followed by a shared-verifier safeguard.

The study asks four questions:

1. How much released gold evidence is reachable at the actual packet depths?
2. Which probability maps correct the imbalance-induced calibration error?
3. Does calibrated expected-F1 selection improve on a fixed top-k rule and a calibrated threshold?
4. Do evidence-set gains survive probability, modality, packet-width, and retrieval perturbations, and do they preserve verdict quality under a shared verifier?

Isotonic, Platt, and prior-shift maps all reduce Brier error sharply. With prior-aware calibration, expected-F1 improves on fixed top-5 by 0.0085 evidence F1 and returns 3.50 items on average. The fitted size and redundancy penalties are zero in every deterministic partition, so neither changes the result. A calibrated threshold performs better, improving on fixed top-5 by 0.0133. Both adaptive rules remain constrained by a packet oracle of only 0.3560 F1.

This paper makes four contributions:

- a retrieval-conditioned evaluation that reports operating-depth recall, packet-oracle F1, complete-gold coverage, and unreachable claims;
- a controlled comparison of six calibration maps across balanced and unweighted multimodal membership scorers;
- a claim-aligned comparison of fixed, threshold, count, and expected-F1 selection, including a reachability-stratified error analysis, a shared-verifier safeguard, and controlled perturbations; and
- an auditable implementation with frozen data revisions, saved candidate probabilities, independent metric recomputation, and a clean deterministic rerun.

Expected-F1 selection can outperform a fixed evidence budget when its inputs are calibrated to the deployment prior. In these experiments, calibrated thresholding performs better, and modest probability distortion removes the expected-F1 gain. Evaluations of adaptive evidence selection therefore need to report calibration and reachability as experimental variables.

2. RELATED WORK

Table caption: Comparison with evidence-aware multimodal verification and retrieval studies. "Adaptive" denotes a variable-size evidence-set output; reranking, filtering, and modality routing alone do not constitute adaptive set decoding.

2.1 Evidence-Based Fact-Checking and Evaluation

Evidence-based verification is commonly decomposed into claim analysis, evidence retrieval, evidence assessment, and verdict prediction [CITE: zeng2021automated,zhou2020survey,islam2020survey]. Dataset surveys show that results depend on domain, label construction, modality coverage, and controls for event or source overlap [CITE: dulizia2021datasets,shah2022systematic,rastogi2022threet]. Cross-domain transfer, incomplete explanations, and inconsistent benchmark protocols remain recurring concerns [CITE: mridha2021review,aimeur2023review,lv2025survey]. Our evaluation therefore follows the retrieval-and-selection chain separately from content-only classification.

Text benchmarks expose complementary evidence requirements. SciFact uses sentence evidence from scientific papers, HoVer requires multi-document reasoning, COVID-Fact follows changing public-health claims, and X-Fact introduces multilingual variation [CITE: wadden2020scifact,jiang2020hover,saakyan2021covidfact,gupta2021xfact]. External-document systems reduce dependence on stylistic shortcuts, but they also introduce retrieval, freshness, and attribution errors [CITE: zhang2023realtime]. We therefore keep evidence-set quality separate from verdict accuracy.

2.2 Multimodal Misinformation Detection

Content-based multimodal detectors model image-text interactions using bilinear pooling, hierarchical attention, topic memory, cross-attention, ambiguity learning, and multi-level fusion [CITE: kumari2021amfb,meel2021han,ying2021topicmemory,ying2021crossattention,chen2022cafe,jing2023mpfn]. Later systems add contrastive, within-modality, stylometric, or semantic-alignment signals [CITE: yan2024fmc,zhu2024ifis,nadeem2023ssm,uppada2022imagetext]. These designs improve post-level classification, but their fused representation does not itself identify which external document or image supports a verdict.

Recent work addresses local-global mismatch, cross-modal knowledge distillation, contextual inconsistency, semantic-space alignment, and conflicting unimodal signals [CITE: wu2023mfir,hu2024matching,peng2024contextual,shang2026semantic,wang2024neutralization]. Out-of-context benchmarks such as NewsCLIPpings, COSMOS, and VERITE test misleading image-caption pairings while controlling unimodal bias [CITE: luo2021newsclippings,aneja2023cosmos,papadopoulos2024verite]. DGM4, SNIFFER, MMDFND, and IMFND broaden localization, explanation, domain coverage, or large vision-language-model use [CITE: shao2023dgm4,qi2024sniffer,tong2024mmdfnd,jiang2025imfnd]. Their main target remains manipulation or veracity rather than an auditable set of external evidence identifiers.

2.3 Multimodal Evidence Retrieval and Verification

MOCHEG provides a shared benchmark for multimodal retrieval, three-way verdict prediction, and explanation generation [CITE: yao2023mocheg]. M-RAV combines dense retrieval, image-text consistency verbalization, relevance estimation, and language-model verification [CITE: luu2027mrav]; MEVER constructs a text-image evidence graph and performs token- and evidence-level fusion [CITE: zhang2026mever]. RED-DOT detects and reranks external multimodal evidence before verdict prediction [CITE: papadopoulos2025reddot]. Related work distinguishes utility from semantic similarity and learns when visual retrieval is necessary [CITE: luo2026utility,tsoneva2026selective]. Fixed retrieval, relevance filtering, modality routing, utility ranking, and adaptive set decoding solve different decision problems.

AVerImaTeC evaluates real-world image-text claims using open-web or organizer-provided knowledge; its shared task conditions verdict scoring on evidence quality [CITE: cao2026averimatecshared]. REVEAL uses iterative sparse and dense retrieval in that setting [CITE: tariq2026reveal], while MMCV adds multi-hop text, image, and table evidence [CITE: wang2025mmcv]. Their question-answer evidence units and access conditions differ from MOCHEG's stable item corpus, so they require a separate transfer protocol.

2.4 Scientific and Citation-Grounded Evidence

Scientific verification offers stable evidence locations in passages, tables, and figures. SciVer contains expert-annotated claim-evidence instances from scientific papers; MuSciClaims focuses on figure-grounded claims and controlled reasoning perturbations; and M^2-TabFact covers tables presented across text and images [CITE: wang2025sciver,lal2025musciclaims,zhou2025m2tabfact]. These resources support precise localization analysis, although their evidence and claim distributions do not directly represent political or breaking-news verification.

An explanation may be fluent while citing the wrong source. Evidence-rationale benchmarks evaluate whether a prediction is tied to a selected span [CITE: deyoung2020eraser], and faithful-by-construction rationalizers restrict predictors to explicit evidence subsets [CITE: jain2020faithful]. Citation-grounded generation measures whether retrieved sources support generated statements [CITE: gao2023alce]; RAGTruth catalogues hallucinations that persist despite retrieval [CITE: niu2024ragtruth]; and citation masking tests whether a fact-checking explanation recovers the correct evidence [CITE: xing2025attribution]. The present study uses released evidence identifiers instead of generated rationales, enabling exact set precision, recall, F1, Jaccard similarity, and exact match.

2.5 Calibration and Adaptive Set Decisions

A score can rank candidates correctly while remaining a poor probability. Temperature scaling, Platt mapping, and nonparametric calibration address different distortion families [CITE: guo2017calibration]. Prior correction is particularly important after class weighting because the optimized training distribution differs from the operating distribution. Expected-utility decoding then relies on the calibrated membership distribution, whereas fixed top-k, thresholding, and learned-count policies use the same scores in structurally different ways.

The encoder, retrieval model, evidence graph, and verifier remain fixed in this study. The experiment tests packet reachability, prior-aware probability correction, and exact expected-F1 set decoding under matched candidates, representations, tuning roles, and downstream verification. This design isolates when adaptive evidence sizing helps and when a calibrated threshold is sufficient (Table [REF: tab:related-comparison]).

3. PROBLEM DEFINITION AND DATA

3.1 Task

For claim q, let G_q be its released complete-gold evidence set and let retrieval produce an ordered packet

Equation: C_q = (c_q1, ..., c_q60).

containing at most 40 text and 20 image candidates. A selector returns S_q ⊆ C_q. The primary claim-level score is

Equation: F1(S_q, G_q) = 2 |S_q ∩ G_q| / (|S_q| + |G_q|).

We macro-average Eq. [REF: eq:set-f1] over claims. Precision, recall, Jaccard similarity, exact-set match, and selected count are secondary evidence metrics.

The primary population includes only claims for which the released annotation defines a nonempty complete-gold set. Claims without such a mapping remain in retrieval construction and receive no perfect oracle score. Assigning them an oracle F1 of one would inflate the apparent packet ceiling.

3.2 Dataset and Population Flow

MOCHEG contains image-text claims, separate text and image evidence, and verdict labels [CITE: yao2023mocheg]. We pin the released train, validation, corpus, and sealed-test revisions by content hash. The official test annotations remain unopened.

All 13,159 released train and validation claims are encoded for retrieval. Of these, 10,970 have a nonempty released complete-gold mapping and define the eligible evidence-set population. The reported selector comparison uses the 1,490 released validation claims as one locked evaluation set. The remaining released claims are assigned to fit, calibration, model-selection, and internal-holdout roles by claim-grouped hashing. Roles are regenerated independently for five deterministic partition seeds: 13, 37, 73, 101, and 137.

3.3 Reachability

Two oracle summaries define the retrieval boundary. The packet oracle returns C_q ∩ G_q for each eligible claim. Its F1 is averaged only over claims with nonempty gold. Complete-gold coverage is the fraction of eligible claims satisfying G_q ⊆ C_q. We also count eligible claims for which C_q ∩ G_q=empty set.

These quantities answer different questions. Oracle F1 measures the best evidence set attainable from the packet. Complete-gold coverage asks whether exact recovery is possible. The zero-reachable count identifies claims on which every selector must receive zero recall. None of these measures credits a selector for evidence that retrieval did not expose.

3.4 Leakage Controls

Candidate rankings are preserved before joining gold identifiers. Evidence labels are used only after candidate construction to fit the membership scorer, calibration map, and selector hyperparameters within their assigned roles. The locked validation set is excluded from every fitted component. The formal test label files are outside the execution path and their hashes are checked before and after each run.

4. METHOD

4.1 Fixed Retrieval Packet

Text retrieval combines BGE-M3 dense sentence similarity with BM25 using reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF) [CITE: cormack2009rrf]. A pinned BGE reranker scores the fused text candidates. Image retrieval combines SigLIP2 image-text similarity with dense similarity between the claim and normalized image filenames, again using RRF. The exact model repositories and immutable revisions are recorded in the frozen configuration; the operational model cards are BGE-M3, (URL: https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-m3) BGE-reranker-v2-m3, (URL: https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3) and SigLIP2. (URL: https://huggingface.co/google/siglip2-base-patch16-224) The retained packet contains the highest-ranked 40 text and 20 image items. Every selector receives this identical packet.

4.2 Membership Scorers

For each claim-candidate pair, the stored feature vector contains the final score and its square, dense and lexical or filename scores, RRF and cross-encoder scores, reciprocal ranks, modality indicators, token-count features, available-candidate count, and duplicate-group size. Features are standardized using fitting-role statistics. A logistic scorer then estimates

Equation: q_qi = sigma(w^T ((x_qi - mu) / s) + b).

where q_qi is a ranking score rather than an assumed operating probability. We compare three organizations: one shared text-image model, separate modality models, and a shared model using the complete feature vector including cross-encoder scores. Each organization is trained with balanced and unweighted binary cross-entropy plus L_2 regularization. Balanced loss is useful for ranking rare evidence, but its output reflects an altered effective class prior and cannot be treated as calibrated.

Let z_qi=logit(q_qi). A calibration map g_m converts it to the membership probability used by the decision policy,

Equation: p_qi = g_m(z_qi) ≈ P(c_qi in G_q | x_qi).

with a separate fitted map for each modality. Six maps are compared:

1. identity, which leaves the raw sigmoid probability unchanged;
2. temperature scaling, sigma(z/T);
3. prior shift, which fits an intercept correction;
4. Platt mapping, sigma(az+b);
5. beta calibration, which is linear in log p and log(1-p); and
6. isotonic regression, a monotone nonparametric map.

Calibration parameters are fitted on the calibration role. The scorer and map are chosen by average precision and Brier score on the separate model-selection role. We report Brier score, negative log likelihood (NLL), expected calibration error (ECE), mean predicted probability, empirical prevalence, and Brier skill relative to the prevalence-only predictor.

This separation prevents a calibrator from being selected on the locked-validation outcome. Average precision screens the scorer's ordering, while Brier score selects the probability map used by threshold and expected-utility decisions. The distinction is important: any monotone map preserves ranking but may substantially change the selected set.

4.3 Evidence-Set Policies

Figure [REF: fig:policy-decoding] isolates the only changing operation: how one shared calibrated ranking is converted into an evidence set.

Figure caption: Decision policies applied to the same calibrated ranking. Fixed top-5 uses one global size; thresholding uses an absolute cutoff; learned count predicts the size; expected-F1 evaluates every prefix and returns the shortest utility-maximizing one. The displayed values illustrate the procedure; they are not measurements.

Fixed top-k.

We tune k in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60] and select the top k candidates. The resulting control is top-5.

Calibrated threshold.

For threshold tau, the selector returns every item with p_qi>=tau, subject to the common packet limit. The threshold is selected from [0.1, ..., 0.9].

Learned count.

A count model predicts the number of evidence items, after which the corresponding probability-ranked prefix is returned.

Expected-F1 decoding.

Candidates are sorted by calibrated probability. For each prefix length k, a dynamic program computes the exact expected F1 under independent Bernoulli membership variables. The selected size is

Equation: k_q* = arg max over 0 <= k <= 60 of E[F1(S_q,k, Y_q) | p_q1:60].

For prefix S_q,k, the utility can be written

Equation: U_q(k) = E[2 T_q,k / (k + T_q,k + R_q,k) | p_q1:60].

where T_q,k is the number of relevant items inside the prefix and R_q,k is the number outside it. Two Poisson-binomial recurrences compute their distributions and the expectation exactly. Ties retain the shorter prefix. This rule uses the full membership distribution rather than a single cutoff or a separately predicted count.

We also test two extensions. The first subtracts lambda k from expected utility. The second additionally subtracts gamma R(S_q,k), where R counts same-source redundancy. Size penalties span 0 to 0.10 and redundancy penalties span 0 to 0.005. These terms are retained as ablations because the fitted optima reveal whether extra decoder structure contributes beyond calibration.

4.4 Decision Scope and Assumptions

The comparison changes only the set decision. Candidate identities, ranks, feature values, scorer outputs, and validation claims are shared across policies. Expected-F1 assumes conditionally independent Bernoulli memberships and is restricted to prefixes of the frozen ranking; it neither reorders candidates nor retrieves a missing item. The released complete-gold mapping is treated as the evaluation target, although Sec. [REF: sec:limitations] discusses possible annotation incompleteness. These boundaries distinguish a decoder error from a retrieval error and prevent an adaptive policy from receiving a more favorable candidate universe.

4.5 Shared Verifier

Evidence policies can improve set overlap while harming the downstream verdict. To isolate that possibility, one verifier architecture and training procedure is shared across policies. Only the selected evidence changes. We compare verdict macro-F1 on the same 1,490 claims and use a declared non-inferiority margin of -0.01 for expected-F1 relative to fixed top-5.

5. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

5.1 Frozen Protocol

The configuration was frozen on 4 November 2026 before the outcome-bearing run. It records data and model revisions, five claim-grouped partition seeds, retrieval depths, scorer families, all calibration candidates, selector grids, evaluation metrics, bootstrap settings, and stopping rules. Prior-aware maps were included because class weighting changes the effective training prior, which a temperature-only map cannot generally repair. No locked-validation outcome was used to choose among the calibration maps.

The configuration permits 36 scorer-calibrator combinations: three scorer families, two loss weightings, and six calibration maps. Screening results are excluded from scientific evidence. Final model choice uses only the assigned training roles, after which every selected configuration is evaluated once on locked validation.

5.2 Baselines and Outcomes

The selector controls are fixed top-5, calibrated thresholding, and learned count prediction. Expected-F1, size-penalized expected-F1, and size-plus-redundancy expected-F1 are the adaptive set-utility variants. The primary outcome is claim-macro complete-gold evidence F1. We also report precision, recall, Jaccard similarity, exact-set match, selected count, calibration metrics, and verdict macro-F1.

For each method comparison, claim-level differences are first averaged over the five deterministic partitions for a given validation claim. A claim-aligned bootstrap then resamples the 1,490 claims. Reported selector intervals use 10,000 samples. Familywise comparisons against fixed top-5 use Holm correction. Robustness intervals use 5,000 samples and are descriptive.

After the primary comparison, we stratify validation claims by packet reachability: no released gold item reachable, some but not all reachable, or complete gold reachable. This diagnostic is explicitly post hoc and descriptive. It localizes the source of aggregate gains without changing the fitted models or the declared decision rule.

The declared superiority condition requires a mean evidence-F1 gain of at least 0.015 over the best fixed rule, a positive effect in every partition, a corrected interval lower bound above zero, calibrated Brier performance better than the prevalence predictor, a reasonable selected-to-gold size ratio, verdict non-inferiority, and exact clean reproduction. All conditions must hold before the selector can be considered superior at the system level.

5.3 Robustness Matrix

We rerun selection under narrower packets of 10 and 20 items, the full retained width, text-only and image-only packets, deterministic removal of 25% and 50% of reachable gold, promotion of five distractors, duplication of the top three items, and probability-temperature perturbations of 0.5 and 2.0. Perturbations leave the fitted selector unchanged. They measure dependence on reachability, modality, ranking contamination, duplication, and probability scale.

5.4 Audit and Reproduction

Every run saves candidate-level scores, calibrated probabilities, selected identifiers, claim-level metrics, selected hyperparameters, environment metadata, and formal-test access flags. An independent audit recomputes population sizes, calibration summaries, set metrics, paired effects, and guard conditions from saved records. A clean rerun writes to a new directory and compares core artifacts by SHA-256 rather than relying only on aggregate agreement.

The reporting and selector analyses use Python 3.13.14 and NumPy 2.5.4 on Windows 11. The lightweight scorers and verifier are implemented with deterministic numerical routines; the five partition seeds govern claim-role assignment rather than stochastic neural training. Pretrained retrieval revisions, packet depths, and all decoding grids are pinned in the recorded configuration.

6.1 Retrieval Defines a Low Ceiling

At the selector's actual operating depths, complete-gold recall is 0.3193 for text at 40 candidates and 0.2663 for images at 20 candidates (Table [REF: tab:operating-retrieval]). The combined packet has an oracle evidence F1 of 0.3560. Only 4.39% of the 10,970 eligible claims have every released gold item in the packet, and 3,086 have no reachable gold item. Exact recovery is therefore impossible for most claims before set decoding begins.

The wider retrieval audit also clarifies which components help. Dense-BM25 RRF reaches text Recall@50 of 0.3224. Cross-encoder reranking raises it to 0.3338 but lowers early Recall@5 from 0.1772 to 0.1335. For images, SigLIP2-filename RRF reaches Recall@50 of 0.3149, compared with 0.2183 for SigLIP2 alone and 0.2227 for filename retrieval alone. Image fusion improves retrieval, while the text reranker trades early ordering for deeper recall.

Table [REF: tab:retrieval-matrix] shows that the text and image branches fail differently. Text RRF improves both MAP and nDCG over either first-stage component, but the cross-encoder gains deeper Recall@50 at the cost of early precision-sensitive ordering. Image fusion improves every reported cutoff and both ranking metrics, yet its Recall@50 remains below the text branch. Consequently, a larger packet alone would recover some missing evidence, but it would also expose the selector to a harder distractor distribution.

The post hoc validation stratification in Table [REF: tab:reachability-strata] exposes where adaptive selection helps. On the 79 claims whose complete released-gold set is present, expected-F1 reaches 0.5240 F1, compared with 0.3593 for fixed top-5 and 0.4237 for thresholding. On the 1,200 partially reachable claims, expected-F1 reaches 0.1839 and does not improve on fixed top-5 at 0.1841; thresholding is strongest at 0.1965. All methods score zero on the 211 claims with no reachable gold. The aggregate expected-F1 gain is therefore concentrated in the small complete-reachability stratum, while thresholding handles incomplete packets better.

6.2 Prior-Aware Maps Repair Calibration

Table [REF: tab:calibration] compares maps on locked validation, averaged across modalities and deterministic partitions. Identity and temperature scaling produce Brier scores of 0.0847 and 0.0832. Their small difference is consistent with a prior error that a slope-only temperature cannot fix. Isotonic, Platt, and prior-shift calibration reduce Brier score to approximately 0.033 while also improving NLL and ECE. Relative to identity, isotonic calibration reduces Brier error by about 61% and ECE from 0.1314 to 0.0063. The selected scorer-map pair beats the prevalence-only Brier reference in all five partitions, with partition-level Brier skill of roughly 14%.

Figure caption: Reliability on locked validation. Temperature scaling remains overconfident because it cannot repair the class-prior shift. Prior-shift, Platt, and isotonic maps track the observed membership rate much more closely.

Scorer choice also changes after absolute calibration is enforced. Modality-specific balanced models are selected for partitions 13 and 37; an unweighted cross-encoder model is selected for 73; and unweighted modality-specific models are selected for 101 and 137. Text maps vary among isotonic, Platt, and beta, whereas image scoring selects isotonic in all five partitions. This variation supports treating scorer family, loss weighting, and calibration as one selection problem.

6.3 Adaptive Selection Helps, but Thresholding Wins

Table [REF: tab:selector-means] gives the main locked-validation results. Fixed top-5 reaches 0.1673 evidence F1. Expected-F1 reaches 0.1759 while selecting only 3.50 items per claim. Its precision rises from 0.2511 to 0.3725, while recall falls from 0.1753 to 0.1483. Expected-F1 trades recall for higher precision and a smaller review set.

Calibrated thresholding reaches the highest F1, 0.1807, and selects 5.51 items. Learned count prediction selects 31.90 items and drops to 0.1100 F1. Predicting size without a well-matched set objective is not competitive here.

The expected-F1 effect is consistent across the partition-level results in Table [REF: tab:partition-selector-results]. Its gain over fixed top-5 ranges from +0.0076 to +0.0101, selected size ranges from 3.43 to 3.59, and the precision-recall profile is stable. Averaged over partitions, the method returns about 30% fewer items than fixed top-5 and improves F1 by about 5.1% relative. Thresholding still wins every partition, so stability does not overturn the baseline ordering.

The claim-aligned effects in Table [REF: tab:selector-effects-detail] and Fig. [REF: fig:selector-effects] confirm these differences. Expected-F1 improves on fixed top-5 by 0.00851 with a 95% interval of [0.00302,0.01400] and Holm-adjusted p=0.0078. Thresholding improves by 0.01334 [0.00931,0.01744]. Its direct advantage over expected-F1 is 0.00483 [0.00003,0.00957].

Figure caption: Claim-aligned evidence-F1 effects relative to fixed top-5. Points are mean differences and bars are 95% intervals.

Both penalty extensions exactly match plain expected-F1. The selected size penalty is zero and the selected redundancy penalty is zero in every partition. Their identical claim-level outputs show that the extra terms add complexity without changing the result on this packet. The measurable improvement comes from calibrated probabilities.

The evidence superiority condition is not met. Although expected-F1 has a positive effect in every partition and its corrected interval excludes zero, its mean gain of 0.00851 is below the declared 0.015 target. The experiment therefore establishes a small positive development effect without meeting the superiority threshold.

6.4 The Verifier Safeguard Remains Unresolved

Under the shared verifier, expected-F1 minus fixed top-5 has a mean verdict macro-F1 difference of +0.00099, with a 95% interval of [-0.01053,0.01279]. The lower endpoint falls just below the declared -0.01 non-inferiority margin. The point estimate is close to zero, but the interval does not establish preservation of verdict quality. Threshold-selected evidence has the highest verifier macro-F1 in every deterministic partition (Table [REF: tab:verifier-effects]).

6.5 Robustness Exposes Calibration Sensitivity

Expected-F1 retains a positive effect under width 20 and 40, text-only evidence, and deterministic gold removal. It loses slightly to fixed top-5 on image-only packets. Promoting five distractors produces a large apparent gain because fixed top-5 collapses when all five leading positions are contaminated; this stress test should not be read as a typical operating estimate.

Probability perturbations change the result most clearly. Sharpening probabilities with temperature 0.5 changes the effect to -0.0193, and flattening them with temperature 2.0 changes it to -0.0221. Both intervals exclude zero. Expected-F1 is therefore sensitive to probability scale even though candidate order is unchanged.

Retrieval sharply limits attainable performance, and prior-aware maps correct the dominant calibration error. Calibrated expected-F1 provides a small precision-oriented gain over fixed top-5, driven primarily by claims with complete reachable gold. Calibrated thresholding is stronger overall. The adaptive advantage depends on probability quality and does not yet satisfy the verifier safeguard.

7. DISCUSSION

7.1 Calibration Changes the Scientific Conclusion

Expected-F1 decoding requires probabilities with an operational meaning: an input of 0.30 should correspond to approximately a 30% chance of membership. Balanced logistic training improves attention to rare positives, but its intercept reflects the reweighted training distribution. Temperature scaling changes the slope of the logits and leaves this intercept problem largely intact. Prior-shift, Platt, and isotonic maps restore the low deployment prevalence and substantially change the selected set size.

Calibration changes the direction of the evidence-F1 comparison. It also explains why the learned size and redundancy penalties become unnecessary: once probabilities reflect the rare membership rate, the unpenalized utility already prefers short prefixes. Evaluations of adaptive selection should report absolute calibration against a prevalence reference and the change from the model's uncalibrated score.

7.2 Why Thresholding Is the Strong Baseline

Expected-F1 uses the joint distribution of all candidate memberships, but its independence approximation and complete-gold objective need not match the empirical data. A tuned threshold directly exploits the same calibrated probabilities with fewer assumptions. Here it obtains more recall with moderate set growth and beats expected-F1 by 0.00483 F1.

Future evaluations need a calibrated-threshold baseline. Beating a fixed budget is insufficient when the same probabilities support a stronger threshold rule. An adaptive decoder should exceed that rule, provide a different precision-cost operating point, or improve a downstream outcome. Expected-F1 provides the second: it returns about two fewer items than thresholding and has substantially higher precision, which may matter when human review time is scarce.

The reachability strata sharpen this interpretation. Expected-F1 is strongest when the packet already contains the complete released-gold set, where pruning a coherent short prefix is feasible. Its advantage disappears when the packet contains only part of the gold set. Thresholding is better in that larger stratum because it tolerates a modestly larger output and recovers more of the available evidence. Because this stratification was post hoc, it diagnoses a possible mechanism but cannot support a prespecified subgroup claim. It does suggest a concrete design target: selection should account for the probability that retrieval is incomplete.

7.3 Retrieval Is the Larger Bottleneck

The packet oracle of 0.3560 places the observed selector scores in context. More than one quarter of eligible claims have no reachable released gold item, and complete-gold coverage is below 5%. Decoder improvements operate within a narrow attainable range. Image-only results are especially weak, and the image-only expected-F1 effect is slightly negative.

The retrieval audit suggests several next steps. Image fusion performs better than either image signal alone. Text cross-encoder reranking improves deeper recall but harms the first few ranks. A ranker trained to optimize early complete-gold coverage, a claim-adaptive retrieval budget, or a selector that explicitly models packet completeness may produce more value than another selection penalty. The fixed packet supports this diagnosis because retrieval and decoding are measured separately.

7.4 Evidence Metrics and Verdict Metrics Differ

Evidence F1 and verdict macro-F1 answer related but distinct questions. The positive evidence effect does not establish verifier non-inferiority. The interval misses the margin narrowly, which may reflect limited validation size, a weak shared verifier, or genuinely different information content in the shorter sets. A larger or stronger verifier study is needed before claiming an end-to-end improvement.

For deployed fact-checking, a useful operating report should include evidence precision and recall, review-set size, retrieval reachability, calibration, and verdict performance. A single aggregate score hides the tradeoff between concise evidence and downstream sufficiency.

7.5 What the Study Supports

The evidence supports three claims. Prior-aware calibration is necessary for adaptive probability-based evidence selection under imbalance. Calibrated expected-F1 can improve complete-gold evidence F1 over a fixed top-5 rule while returning fewer items, mainly when the retrieved packet contains the full released-gold set. Calibrated thresholding is the best tested policy on this development population. The evidence does not support a claim of end-to-end superiority, a benefit from size or redundancy penalties, or generalization beyond the evaluated MOCHEG setting.

8. LIMITATIONS AND ETHICS

8.1 Limitations

First, the reported selector study uses released MOCHEG validation claims. The set was locked for this run, but earlier analysis in the broader project had already used the same development resource. The result is a controlled reanalysis; fresh external confirmation remains pending. The official MOCHEG test annotations remain sealed.

Second, complete-gold annotations may be incomplete with respect to all valid web evidence. Exact-set metrics reward agreement with the released mapping and can mark a plausible alternative as wrong. The reachability-stratified analysis is post hoc, and its complete-reachability result is based on 79 validation claims. It provides a mechanism diagnosis that requires prespecified replication and cannot support a population-level subgroup guarantee.

Third, the membership scorers are deliberately lightweight to preserve experimental control and reproducibility. They are not intended as state-of-the-art representation learners. Better multimodal rerankers could change both ranking and calibration. Isotonic regression can also be data-hungry; its repeated selection for images should be tested on another corpus.

The saved records identify the software environment and deterministic analysis runtime, but they do not provide a complete end-to-end compute, latency, or energy profile for embedding and retrieval. The study therefore makes no efficiency claim beyond the number of evidence items returned to a reviewer.

Fourth, the expected-F1 calculation assumes conditionally independent candidate memberships. Evidence items from the same source, duplicate images, and complementary sentences violate this assumption. The tested redundancy penalty did not help, but that does not rule out a learned structured model.

Fifth, the robustness conditions are controlled diagnostics and cover only a small part of possible production drift. The temperature perturbations demonstrate sensitivity to probability scale; they do not estimate how often such distortion occurs in a deployed system.

Finally, external datasets differ materially. AVerImaTeC uses web or curated question-answer evidence, while MMCV includes tables and multi-hop support. Porting the selector requires a new definition of evidence units, a new calibration study, and an evaluation that respects each resource's access conditions.

8.2 Ethical Considerations

Automated fact-checking can create unwarranted confidence, particularly when retrieval misses relevant evidence. The interface for any deployment should expose source identifiers, retrieval limitations, probability calibration status, and uncertainty. The system should assist trained reviewers rather than silently replace them.

Data and model artifacts may inherit geographic, linguistic, and source-selection biases. MOCHEG is not a representative sample of all claims or communities. Performance should not be generalized to low-resource languages or sensitive domains without targeted evaluation. Web evidence may also contain personal, disturbing, or copyrighted material; access and display should follow the source terms and minimize unnecessary reproduction.

The formal test remains sealed, failed or unfavorable outcomes are retained, and all reported comparisons include uncertainty. These controls reduce selective reporting and make it easier to distinguish a useful development finding from a deployment claim.

9. CONCLUSION

We evaluated adaptive multimodal evidence selection as a probability-dependent decision inside a fixed retrieval pipeline. Prior-aware calibration reduces the dominant probability error and allows expected-F1 to reach 0.1759 evidence F1 while returning 3.50 items per claim, a gain of 0.0085 over fixed top-5. The gain is concentrated in claims whose complete released-gold set is reachable. Calibrated thresholding remains stronger overall at 0.1807, while size and redundancy penalties add no measurable value. The shared verifier does not establish non-inferiority, and probability perturbations reverse the adaptive gain. Evidence-selector studies should therefore report three boundaries together: what retrieval makes reachable, whether membership scores are calibrated to the operating prior, and whether a structured decision rule improves on a calibrated threshold. In this setting, calibration adds value, extra decoder penalties do not, and retrieval remains the dominant limitation.
