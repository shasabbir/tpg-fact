Multimodal Misinformation Detection with
Calibrated Abstention

Abstract—Web evidence can mislead a multimodal
fact-checker when it was published too late, copied
across several sites, or taken from sources that depend
on one another. TPG-Fact addresses these problems
by enforcing a decision-time cutoff and grouping syndicated documents before aggregation. It represents
claims, documents, images, publishers, links, and publication order in a heterogeneous graph. A hierarchical
classifier first estimates whether the retrieved material
is sufficient. When it is, a second head predicts whether
the claim is supported or refuted; otherwise, the system returns insufficient evidence. Temperature scaling
and a validation-selected threshold support selective
abstention. The accompanying US-TPMFC benchmark
uses United States ClaimReview cases and evidence
recovered from open web archives. Training covers
2020–2023, validation uses 2024, and testing uses 2025–
2026, with related events, near-duplicate claims, and
reused images kept within one partition. The evaluation
compares the method with matched multimodal retrieval baselines and tests temporal leakage, syndicated
evidence, calibration, and selective risk.

We ask whether time-valid evidence from independent
sources improves future-event misinformation detection
and reduces overconfident decisions relative to ordinary
multimodal RAG. Each case is an English image–text
claim about a United States event. The system returns
supported, refuted, or insufficient evidence, together with
the evidence identifiers and cutoff used for the decision.
The study design has four parts. US-TPMFC provides
a temporal benchmark built from professional fact-check
metadata and open archives. A provenance graph records
publication order, duplication, hyperlinks, publishers, entities, events, and shared media. The detector evaluates
evidence sufficiency before it predicts veracity. The evaluation then measures future-event performance, temporal
leakage, sensitivity to copied sources, calibration, and
selective risk.

II. Related Work
A. Multimodal Misinformation Detection
NewsCLIPpings introduced a large benchmark of authentic news images paired with deliberately mismatched
captions [6]. VERITE later addressed unimodal bias and
topic imbalance in real-world evaluation [7]. KAMP uses
single-modality, cross-modal, and knowledge-content objectives during multimodal pretraining [1]. Other work
uses synthetic examples to improve transfer to real misinformation cases [8]. Event-Radar represents visual manipulation, textual emotion, and event-level inconsistency
in an event graph [9]. These methods provide the main
no-retrieval and event-aware baselines for our study.

I. Introduction
An authentic photograph can still mislead when it is
paired with the wrong caption, date, place, or event.
Models that focus on manipulation artifacts or surfacelevel image–text compatibility may miss this kind of false
context. External evidence can help, but it creates its own
problems. Some documents appear only after the decision
deadline, search results may repeat one syndicated report,
and a model can sound certain even when the available
evidence is weak.
Several multimodal systems address parts of this problem. KAMP learns from text, images, and background
knowledge graphs [1]. RED-DOT reranks retrieved multimodal evidence by relevance [2], while COVE recovers
an image’s original context before judging its caption
[3]. Other systems use multimodal retrieval-augmented
generation to reason over web sources [4], [5]. None of these

MOCHEG treats multimodal fact-checking as a sequence of evidence retrieval, claim verification, and explanation generation [10]. MetaSumPerceiver summarizes
evidence drawn from several multimodal documents [11].
RED-DOT accounts for irrelevant web evidence through

iterative reasoning [4], [5], and SNIFFER retrieves external material for out-of-context detection [12]. Our method

We treat US-TPMFC as a benchmark only if the filtered
corpus contains at least 2,000 cases, 300 cases per label, 50
test events, and two independent evidence clusters for at
least 70% of retained claims. If the collection misses any
threshold, we use it as a temporal and provenance stress
test instead.

C. Image Context, Time, and Evidence Dependence
The 5Pils system recovers an image’s original context
from web evidence [13], and COVE predicts that context
before assessing a caption [3]. Chronological distribution
shift can reduce the accuracy of multimodal misinformation models [14]. ChronoFact handles complex temporal
claims with structured timeline reasoning [15]. Source
information also affects how RAG-based fact verification
resolves conflicting evidence [16]. We represent publication
time and source dependence in the same evidence graph.

IV. Temporal-Provenance Method
A. Time-Constrained Multimodal Retrieval
For each claim, retrieval begins with named entities,
temporal expressions, OCR text, and an image caption. BM25 and BGE-M3 retrieve text candidates, while
SigLIP2 embeddings and perceptual hashes retrieve images. Reciprocal-rank fusion merges the lexical, dense,
and visual rankings. A reranker processes the top 100
candidates, and the system keeps the 20 highest-ranked
items that satisfy the publication-time cutoff.

A. Task Definition

B. Evidence-Source Provenance Graph

Each instance contains claim text xt , an image xv , a
decision cutoff τ , and an evidence collection E≤τ . The output is y ∈ {supported, refuted, insufficient}. Documents
published after τ are unavailable to the model. The event
is the independent unit for data splitting and uncertainty
analysis.

The graph contains nodes for claims, images, documents, publishers, entities, and events. Typed edges record
retrieval relevance, image containment, near-duplicate
content, hyperlinks, publisher membership, entity or event
mentions, and temporal order. We estimate document
clusters from exact hashes, MinHash-style lexical similarity, semantic similarity, perceptual image distance,
hyperlinks, and publication order. The earliest observed
document is an origin candidate because the available
records may not reveal the original author.
Let C(E) denote the partition of evidence documents
into dependency clusters. The model first aggregates documents within a cluster and then combines information
across clusters. As a result, repeated copies of one report
do not receive the same weight as several independent
sources.

B. Candidate Acquisition
Corpus construction starts with English ClaimReview
records about United States events from 2020–2026.
GDELT provides discovery metadata, and Common Crawl
provides archived page observations. We retain cases with
an identifiable claim time, a recoverable image or archive,
an unambiguous professional rating, and enough timestamp information to enforce the cutoff. The retrieval pool
excludes fact-check domains and documents that contain
the final verdict.

C. Hierarchical Decision and Abstention
C. Label Policy
We normalize only explicit site ratings. True or correct
maps to supported. False, fake, or miscaptioned maps to
refuted. Unproven, unsubstantiated, or no evidence maps
to insufficient evidence. We exclude mixed, partially true,
satire, opinion, and ambiguous ratings. The labels come
from professional fact-checkers; automatically extracted
evidence links are treated as silver evidence.
D. Temporal Partitioning and Release
Training covers 2020–2023, validation uses 2024, and final testing uses 2025–2026. Events, near-duplicate claims,
and images with matching perceptual hashes remain in
one partition. The release package contains URLs, cryptographic and perceptual hashes, timestamps, normalized labels, graph annotations, retrieval queries, and split
manifests. It does not redistribute copyrighted articles or
images.

A heterogeneous graph transformer encodes the
temporal-provenance graph for the claim. The sufficiency
head estimates p(s = 1 | x, E≤τ ). Sufficient evidence
passes to a veracity head that predicts supported or refuted; the remaining cases receive the insufficient-evidence
label. We fit temperature scaling to validation predictions
and choose an abstention threshold on the same split to
target 80% answer coverage. Each prediction records class
probabilities, evidence identifiers, source clusters, and the
enforced cutoff.
V. Experimental Protocol
A. Baselines
The controlled comparison includes majority prediction, text-only classification, image–text fusion without
retrieval, and an open multimodal language model under zero- and few-shot prompting. Retrieval baselines
cover vanilla multimodal RAG, time-filtered RAG without

provenance, provenance-aware RAG without time filtering, and RED-DOT where its implementation is compatible with the protocol. Every method uses the same splits,
candidate corpus, evaluation code, and 20-trial validation
budget.
B. Metrics and Statistical Analysis
The primary metric is macro-F1 on the 2025–2026
event-disjoint test set. Secondary metrics are per-class F1,
balanced accuracy, macro one-versus-rest AUROC, Brier
score, negative log-likelihood, expected calibration error,
area under the risk–coverage curve, risk at 80% coverage, Recall@10, nDCG@10, latency, GPU memory, and
retrieved-document count. Final configurations are evaluated with five seeds. Differences use 10,000 paired eventlevel bootstrap replicates, with Holm correction across the
primary comparison family.
C. Ablation and Stress Tests
The ablations remove temporal filtering, dependency
clustering, temporal edge features, graph aggregation,
text evidence, visual evidence, the sufficiency gate, and
evidence-dropout training one at a time. The stress tests
add syndicated copies and post-cutoff documents that
reveal the verdict. They also remove the earliest observed
source, introduce conflicts between dependent and independent evidence, swap captions, and reuse historical
images.
D. Selection and Falsification Rules
We select hyperparameters and the abstention threshold
using validation data only. Evidence-source provenance
must improve either future-event performance or resistance to duplicate injection to support a performance
claim. Calibrated abstention must reduce selective risk
at matched coverage. Gains that depend on post-cutoff
documents count as leakage.
VI. Evaluation Questions
The first analysis tests whether excluding post-cutoff
documents changes retrieval quality and future-event classification. The full method is compared with vanilla multimodal RAG and with provenance-aware RAG that does
not enforce a time cutoff. Event-level paired bootstrap
intervals quantify the differences.
The second analysis measures how source dependence
affects a verdict. It compares cluster-level aggregation with
document-level aggregation before and after injecting one,
five, and ten syndicated copies of the same source. Changes
in macro-F1, confidence, Brier score, and selective risk
show whether repeated evidence is being counted as independent corroboration.
The third analysis examines the sufficiency gate and
calibrated abstention. A flat three-class classifier and an
uncalibrated confidence threshold provide matched controls. The comparison uses insufficient-evidence F1, area
under the risk–coverage curve, and risk at 80% coverage.

VII. Discussion, Ethics, and Limitations
The main methodological risk is mistaking repeated
web coverage for independent corroboration. Dependency
clusters can only use observable content, links, domains,
and timestamps. They cannot prove that two publishers
worked independently or identify the original author with
certainty. Archive timestamps show when a page was
observed, which may be later than its first publication.
The corpus inherits professional fact-check labels and
the editorial policies behind them. Restricting the mapping to explicit ratings reduces ambiguity, but it can also
create selection bias and class imbalance. The focus on
United States events and English sources limits geographic
and linguistic generalization. Common Crawl and GDELT
do not cover the whole web, and blocked or deleted pages
may differ systematically from the pages that remain
available.
The release will not redistribute copyrighted articles or
images without permission. It will contain URLs, hashes,
derived metadata, split manifests, and collection code. The
system is meant for research and assisted review, not autonomous censorship or content removal. An insufficientevidence prediction calls for further investigation; it does
not mean that the claim is false.
VIII. Conclusion
This study examines whether a multimodal fact-checker
benefits from knowing when evidence appeared and when
several documents came from the same source. TPG-Fact
enforces a decision-time cutoff, groups dependent evidence
before aggregation, checks whether the retrieved material
is sufficient, and abstains when confidence is too low. The
evaluation separates ordinary future-event performance
from gains caused by temporal leakage and repeated documents. Its ablations also test whether the graph, source
clusters, and sufficiency gate earn their place in the model.
The final claims depend on controlled comparisons; a large
stack of web documents does not count as corroboration
on its own.
