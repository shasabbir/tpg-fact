\section{Introduction}

Automated claim verification increasingly depends on evidence distributed across text, tables, figures, and images. Recent systems retrieve multimodal evidence, fuse it for verdict prediction, and generate natural-language explanations. M-RAV verbalizes image--text alignment before asking a large language model to verify a claim \cite{luu2027mrav}. MEVER learns a multimodal evidence graph and jointly addresses retrieval, verification, and explanation generation \cite{zhang2026mever}. These systems advance end-to-end verification, but a practical output still has two separable properties: whether its verdict is correct and whether its cited evidence set is appropriately sized and relevant.

A correct verdict does not guarantee a useful evidence report. A model may exploit claim wording, dataset artifacts, or an uncited item. Likewise, a fluent explanation can agree with the predicted label while attributing the conclusion to the wrong source. Text-only work has documented attribution errors in generated fact-checking explanations \cite{xing2025attribution}. Multimodal scientific benchmarks further show that even strong foundation models struggle to locate and combine evidence in figures, tables, and text \cite{wang2025sciver,lal2025musciclaims}. Citation evaluation should therefore be explicit rather than inferred from verdict accuracy or prose overlap.

A less visible problem is citation cardinality. Returning the same top $k$ items for every claim can inflate recall by over-citing simple claims and can truncate the evidence for claims that require several items. Learning cardinality jointly with the verifier is not a clean test of this problem because it may also alter the evidence ranking and verdict representation. We instead ask a narrower question: can a citation-count predictor improve the citation set while the verifier and ranking remain exactly fixed?

\method{} first trains a multimodal graph verifier and evidence selector. It then freezes that model and trains a small cardinality head from the sorted selector-score profile and candidate-availability counts. The head chooses how many items to return from the unchanged ranking. The fixed-budget and adaptive outputs are paired by construction: any citation difference is caused by set size, while verdict probabilities and predictions remain identical. Stable evidence identifiers permit direct set-level scoring without interpreting generated prose.

The study makes four contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a decoupled formulation of citation-set sizing in which the verifier, complete evidence ranking, verdict probabilities, and verdict predictions are implementation-checked invariants;
    \item a five-seed, source-paper-grouped evaluation against three matched fixed budgets using 10,000 paired group-bootstrap samples;
    \item the negative finding that improved citation-count accuracy does not imply improved citation macro-F1: \method{} beats top-one and top-three but loses to top-two; and
    \item diagnostic evidence showing that gold cardinality, packet-count features, and a separately trained count head do not overcome errors in the frozen evidence order.
\end{itemize}

All result claims come from the frozen SciVer official-test archive and its independent record-level audit. The failed adaptive-superiority hypothesis is retained rather than replaced by post-test tuning.
