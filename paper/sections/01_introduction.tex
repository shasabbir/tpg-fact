\section{Introduction}

Automated fact-checking is often organized as a pipeline: identify a checkable claim, retrieve potentially relevant sources, assess the evidence, and predict a verdict \cite{zeng2021automated,zhou2020survey}. Evidence-based systems differ from content-only misinformation classifiers because their output must be grounded in identifiable source material rather than inferred only from linguistic style, publisher identity, or the appearance of a post. This distinction makes retrieval and evidence attribution part of the scientific claim: a correct verdict is not fully justified when the cited material is irrelevant or incomplete.

The problem becomes harder when claims and evidence are multimodal. A claim may combine a caption with an image, while decisive evidence may be distributed across a fact-check article, a quoted passage, and one or more images. The modalities may be complementary, redundant, or contradictory, and a system must preserve the identity of each selected item if its justification is to be audited. MOCHEG formalizes this setting as joint text--image evidence retrieval, three-way claim verification, and explanation generation \cite{yao2023mocheg}. Later systems add dense retrieval, image--text verbalization, language-model verification, or graph-based evidence reasoning \cite{luu2027mrav,zhang2026mever}. These advances make evidence selection itself a measurable component rather than an invisible preprocessing step.

A correct verdict and a useful evidence set are related but different outputs. A model can predict the right label while citing irrelevant items, omitting a necessary image, or returning many redundant documents. Generated explanations can also be fluent without accurately attributing their statements to the selected evidence \cite{xing2025attribution,niu2024ragtruth}. Evaluation must therefore preserve the identity of selected items and measure their overlap with released gold evidence instead of treating a verdict or plausible explanation as sufficient.

Most multimodal retrieval systems nevertheless expose a globally fixed top-$k$ set. A single budget is simple and reproducible, but it assumes that every claim needs the same number of items. Choosing a larger $k$ can recover more gold evidence while reducing precision; choosing a smaller $k$ has the opposite effect. Predicting only the gold count does not solve this trade-off when the ranking is imperfect: knowing how many items are needed does not identify which ranked items are correct. A preceding SciVer study in this project isolated citation-set size from verification and obtained a negative result: a learned count head improved count accuracy but did not beat the strongest fixed budget in evidence-set F1. That completed benchmark is used only as motivation and supplies no selection or confirmation evidence for the present study.

This study asks whether the selection rule can target evidence-set utility directly. Calibrated Expected-F1 Evidence Selection (\method) estimates the probability that each candidate belongs to the gold evidence set, orders candidates by that probability, and evaluates every admissible prefix. An exact Poisson-binomial dynamic program computes expected set-F1 under an explicitly stated independent-Bernoulli approximation, and the decoder returns the smallest prefix attaining the maximum expectation. Fixed-budget, threshold, count-prediction, and \method{} outputs use the same candidate packet, frozen features, and scorer family; the end-to-end comparison also uses the same verifier checkpoint. The intended solution therefore changes the set decision while holding retrieval access and downstream model capacity constant.

The evaluation deliberately separates a convenient candidate universe from realistic retrieval. A checked 400-claim feasibility packet first tests the selector on released judgment candidates ranked without relevance labels. The primary development comparison then performs label-blind retrieval from all 91,822 released text documents and 90,270 train/development image paths. Both stages use pinned BGE-M3 and SigLIP2 features, three seeds, matched controls, paired bootstrap analysis, and independently recomputed artifacts. \method{} improves over the selected fixed budget on the restricted packet but loses under full-corpus retrieval in every seed. The primary development gate consequently fails and the untouched official test remains sealed.

The contributions of this development study are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item an adaptive multimodal evidence selector that optimizes exact expected set-F1 rather than citation count or a global retrieval depth;
    \item a matched comparison protocol that shares candidates, representations, scorer inputs, tuning access, and verifier checkpoints across adaptive and fixed selectors;
    \item a test-sealed MOCHEG development evaluation with calibration and evidence-count controls; and
    \item an auditable development gate that prevents the official test from being consumed when the proposed selector does not establish a meaningful development advantage; and
    \item empirical evidence that a selector conclusion obtained from a judgment-candidate packet can reverse under label-blind full-corpus retrieval.
\end{itemize}

The paper therefore does not claim empirical superiority. Its central finding is that candidate construction and retrieval error materially change the conclusion about adaptive evidence selection: the initially positive packet result does not survive the end-to-end development setting. Reporting this reversal, including the failed seeds and preserved artifacts, is necessary to distinguish a selector mechanism result from a realistic retrieval result.
