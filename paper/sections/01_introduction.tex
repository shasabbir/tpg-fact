\section{Introduction}

There are several approaches to automated fact-checking, many of which can be implemented as a pipeline: finding a claim that can be fact-checked, finding potential evidence sources, evaluating evidence and predicting a verdict \cite{zeng2021automated,zhou2020survey}. Evidence-based systems are distinct from classifiers that rely on content alone as they require that the output be backed up by identifiable source material rather than only the linguistic style, the publisher or the appearance of the post. This distinction implies that retrieval and evidence attribution are part of the scientific claim and that a correct verdict is not fully justified when the cited material is irrelevant or incomplete.

When claims and evidence are multimodal, it is more difficult. A claim can combine a caption and an image, while decisive evidence may be distributed across a fact-check article, a quoted passage and one or more images. The modalities can be complementary, redundant or contradictory and a system needs to uphold the identity of every item selected if the reason for the selection is to be audited. MOCHEG materialises this situation as joint text--image evidence retrieval, three-way claim verification, and explanation generation \cite{yao2023mocheg}. Subsequent systems incorporate dense retrieval, visual language, language-model verification, or graph-based evidence reasoning \cite{luu2027mrav,zhang2026mever}. These developments enable us to make evidence selection a measurable component, a step away from the invisible preprocessing step.

The output of a correct verdict and useful evidence set are different but related. A model may produce a correct label, and yet mention irrelevant items or fail to mention an important image or produce many redundant documents. Fluently-generated explanations can also be inaccurate in attributing their statements to the evidence chosen \cite{xing2025attribution,niu2024ragtruth}. Therefore, the evaluation should maintain the identity of the items selected and be based on how much they overlap with the released gold evidence and not be based on a verdict or a plausible explanation.

Most multimodal retrieval systems still present a fixed (universal) set of top-$k$ results. A single budget is easy to reproduce and use, but it assumes that each claim requires the same number of items. The higher the value of $k$, the more gold evidence that can be recovered but with less precision and vice versa. When the ranking is not perfect, predicting just the gold count is not enough to answer this trade-off: knowing how many items are needed does not identify which ranked items are correct. A previous SciVer study in this project found a negative result: a learned count head improved count accuracy but did not beat the strongest fixed budget in evidence-set F1. The above-mentioned completed benchmark is only used as motivation and provides no selection or confirmation evidence for the current study.

The question in this study is whether it is possible to target evidence-set utility directly by the selection rule. Calibrated Expected-F1 Evidence Selection (\method) estimates the probability of each candidate being in the gold evidence set, sorts the candidates by these probabilities and checks each prefix that is admissible. An exact Poisson-binomial dynamic program is used to calculate expected set-F1 under an explicitly stated independent-Bernoulli approximation and the decoder outputs the smallest prefix where the expectation is maximized. For all outputs (fixed-budget, threshold, count-prediction and \method), the same candidate packet, frozen features and scorer family are used; and for end-to-end comparison, the same verifier checkpoint is used. The proposed solution consequently alters the set decision, but preserves retrieval access and the capacity of downstream models.

The evaluation is deliberately split into a convenient candidate universe and realistic retrieval. The checked 400-claim feasibility packet is a first test of the selector on released judgment candidates ranked without relevance labels. The primary development comparison then retrieves label-blind from all 91,822 released text documents and 90,270 train/development image paths. The two stages include pinned BGE-M3 and SigLIP2 features, as well as three seeds, matched controls, paired bootstrap analysis and independently recomputed artifacts. \method{} outperforms the chosen fixed budget in the restricted packet, but is outperformed under full-corpus retrieval in all seeds. As a result, the main development gate fails and the official test, which has not been touched, is still sealed.

This development study's contribution is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a multimodal evidence selector which adaptively optimizes the exact expected set-F1 instead of the number of citations or a global retrieval depth;
    \item a matched comparison protocol, where the same set of candidates, set of representations, inputs by the scorer, accesses to tuning, and checkpoints by the verifier are shared between the adaptive and fixed selectors;
    \item an evaluation, calibration and evidence-count test-sealed MOCHEG development;
    \item an auditable development gate to keep the official test from being used when the proposed selector fails to create a clear development benefit; and
    \item empirical data showing that a selector conclusion extracted from a judgment-candidate packet can reverse under label-blind full-corpus retrieval.
\end{itemize}

So the paper does not claim empirical superiority. It finds that the initial positive result for adaptive evidence selection on the packet does not hold in the end-to-end development environment because of candidate construction and retrieval error. Reporting this reversal, including the failed seeds and preserved artifacts, is important for distinguishing a selector mechanism result from a realistic retrieval result.
