\section{Discussion}

The checked packet experiment supports a narrow conclusion: adaptive calibrated selection substantially outperforms the strongest development-selected fixed budget on the released judgment-candidate packet. It does not isolate the proposed exact decoder as the cause, because a global calibrated threshold is stronger in every seed. The result also does not establish end-to-end retrieval quality because the packet begins from released judgment candidates and the full-corpus comparison is pending.

The two calibration trials illustrate why the gate is conjunctive. Unrestricted temperature scaling slightly worsens Brier score for one seed despite a positive evidence-F1 effect. The declared training-only guard repairs that failure without consulting validation labels. The matched verifier then shows heterogeneous downstream effects: its mean point estimate passes the margin, but seed 13 declines and no interval excludes zero. These observations must remain visible even if later stages pass.

The development gate is deliberately demanding because the formal benchmark can be consumed only once for a confirmatory interpretation. It also prevents a repetition of the preceding count-prediction study's main evaluation error: beating one convenient fixed budget while losing to another reasonable budget. The new protocol selects the comparator on permitted development data and requires a practically relevant effect across all pilot seeds before formal evaluation.

A positive MOCHEG result would still be bounded. It would show that expected-F1 decoding improves evidence selection under the released corpus, labels, and matched system, not that the method universally identifies truth or produces faithful natural-language explanations. A strong journal submission would benefit from a second licensed external benchmark after the MOCHEG protocol is frozen, but such an extension must not contaminate the main test.
